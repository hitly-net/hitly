On 24 November 2026 the FCA published [Our approach to agentic AI](https://www.fca.org.uk/firms/innovation/ai-approach/agentic-ai), setting out what it calls its "expectations for firms, technology providers and social media platforms looking to develop and deploy agentic AI products in the financial services sector". It is not a consultation, and on our reading it does not create new rules. It is, though, the clearest statement yet of where the regulator expects to look when autonomous agents touch retail financial services — and some of it is aimed squarely at companies like ours.

We build HITLy™, an approval inbox for agent work: agents propose actions, humans approve or reject them, and the record of who did what survives afterwards. So we read the statement closely. Here is what it says, in our words.

## What the statement asks for

For firms, the expectations centre on knowing what your agents actually do: understanding the systems you deploy, the degree of autonomy you hand them, the risks they create, and having controls that hold up both in real time and under stress. Accountability, the FCA is clear, stays with the firm and its senior managers — delegating work to an agent does not delegate responsibility for it. The example the statement gives of the record it expects is refreshingly concrete: whether a review of an agent action was required, whether it actually happened, and what its outcome was ([FCA, Our approach to agentic AI](https://www.fca.org.uk/firms/innovation/ai-approach/agentic-ai)). The FCA is also explicit that this is not a one-off review: "We will assess the services firms provide through agentic products", it writes, "including how they develop over time, to ensure they keep to regulatory requirements."

That "over time" clause matters. An agent that behaved itself at launch is not the same system after three model swaps and a new tool grant. Continuous assessment presupposes continuous evidence — which in practice means you need to be able to reconstruct what an agent did, on which instructions, with whose sign-off.

For providers, there is a specific expectation about honesty at the interface: AI providers should help consumers "understand the limits of financial information and advice generated by their models through clear, proportionate and consistent disclaimers", reinforced by effective operational safeguards. In other words: help consumers understand what the model's output is and is not, and do not rely on the disclaimer alone to catch what the model cannot.

## Not a consultation — yet

Two engagement routes are open, and it is worth distinguishing them:

- An **Agentic Policy Sprint**, described as a forum "for firms, policymakers and tech providers to collaborate". It is currently an expression-of-interest stage: you register by emailing the FCA's AI Lab at [AgenticAI@fca.org.uk](mailto:AgenticAI@fca.org.uk). There is no formal consultation document and no stated closing date.
- A recent joint statement with the other regulators in the **Digital Regulation Cooperation Forum** (DRCF) on firms developing and deploying personal AI agents, linked from the same page. If your users' personal agents will transact with your regulated services, that statement is the DRCF's preview of its thinking.

## The October paper carries the detail

The agentic AI statement does not stand alone. On 19 October 2026 the FCA published [AI in financial services: good practice and areas for improvement](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/good-poor-practice/ai-financial-services), an observations paper drawn from supervisory engagement. The good-practice examples that map most directly to agentic deployments:

- **A central inventory of agents.** Firms with agentic use cases registered their agents in a central inventory — what is live, who owns it, what systems and data it can reach, what controls apply — precisely so they could intervene when risks emerged.
- **Governance layers before autonomy.** Firms deploying more autonomous systems designed the governance structure first, including escalation routes and compliance controls that maintained accountability and human oversight across complex workflows.

The improvement areas are the mirror image: informal approvals, generic governance reused from non-AI processes, control functions engaged too late, and undefined risk appetite — firms defaulting to avoiding anything near a regulatory boundary rather than managing toward it.

## Where oversight tooling fits — and where it does not

Reading the two documents together, the pattern is consistent: the regulator wants to see that you know which agents you have, what they are authorised to do, and what happened when they did it — with a named human accountable at the points that matter.

That is the shape of what HITLy does. Agents route consequential actions to a human approval [inbox](/docs/inbox) before they can execute; every proposal, decision, and edit is recorded in a durable [trace](/docs/trace); and [governance controls](/docs/governance) decide what needs approval, by whom, and within what budget. It is designed so that the reconstruction the FCA describes — who approved this, when, on what evidence — is a query rather than an archaeology project.

What HITLy does not do deserves equal space. It does not decide which of your use cases are high-risk, whether your conduct meets Consumer Duty, or what your risk appetite should be. No vendor tool can. The inventory, the governance, and the judgement stay with you and your advisers; HITLy is one way to make the operating evidence exist.

## What we are watching

Three things we will keep an eye on: whether the Agentic Policy Sprint produces anything firmer than expectations; whether the DRCF follows its personal-agents statement with sector-specific guidance; and how quickly "we will assess over time" shows up in actual supervisory questions to firms. If your compliance calendar has an agentic-AI item, the FCA statement is now the first thing to read for it.
